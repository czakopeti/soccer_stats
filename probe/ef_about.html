About

About elofootball.com

elofootball.com rates every European club with the Elo system – the method chess uses to rank players. It was inspired by eloratings.net, which ranks national teams with Elo ratings and, in our view, captures their actual relative strength more accurately than any incarnation of the official FIFA rankings so far.

Scope

This site applies that concept to European club football. It arguably provides more insight than league tables, as it allows the comparison of clubs within a country across all covered competitions. A club which has a mediocre run in its domestic league but excels in international competitions or domestic cups will therefore rank higher than the league table alone suggests.

More interestingly, the system allows the comparison between countries. Clubs involved in international competition exchange points with each other and thus raise or lower the average rating and the relative position of their country.

The database covers 54 countries – all UEFA members except Liechtenstein, which has no league system of its own – plus the former states Czechoslovakia, East Germany, the Soviet Union and Yugoslavia. First divisions are available for all countries, second-tier or lower leagues for 22 of them. Domestic cups, league cups, super cups and relegation play-offs are covered as well. Additionally, the database contains all competitive international matches between eligible clubs of these countries: currently the Champions League, Europa League, Conference League and UEFA Super Cup, and historically the Cup Winners' Cup, Inter-Cities Fairs Cup, Intertoto Cup, Mitropa Cup (until 1959) and Latin Cup, as well as the qualification rounds of these competitions. Which seasons are complete for each country is listed under Data coverage.

Rules

Each league is assigned an initial rating which aims to reflect its long-term strength, e.g. 2000 for Spain, 1700 for Germany and 1625 for the Netherlands. These starting values were found by giving all countries equal values, running several simulations, observing how the countries diverged over time and adjusting accordingly. The actual initial ratings are, however, of secondary importance, since Elo ratings always converge towards the real relative strength of a club (or country): a club with an initial rating of 1000 (2000) which plays n consecutive draws against 1500 clubs will converge towards 1500 with increasing n, because the system rewards (punishes) draws against stronger (weaker) teams. The convergence is even quicker if the stronger (weaker) team loses (wins). Even with an initial rating of only 1000, Spanish clubs would almost always face better-rated competition at first and consequently rise very quickly to a rating that reflects their actual strength.

The database starts in 1955/56, the inaugural season of the competition now known as the Champions League. All initial members of a league are assigned the starting value of that league. A relegated club keeps its rating until it is promoted again, or until it meets another eligible team in a cup competition. Admittedly, this also means that a former first-division club which has since dropped to the fifth tier will still enter a cup match with the rating it had when it was relegated from the first division. A promoted club which was not a founding member of the league starts with the average rating of the teams relegated in the previous season or, if no team was relegated that year, with the rating of the worst team.

Games in domestic cups and international competitions only count if both participants have already played in their domestic first division (or in a lower division covered by the database). Domestic cup games between two former first-division members are included, since both clubs already have a rating.

For simplicity, all seasons run from 1 July to 30 June, even though Sweden, for instance, still plays a calendar-year season, as Russia did until 2011.

In depth: setting the weighting factor

First published 13 September 2017

When I set up the calculation, my goal was to replicate the methodology of eloratings.net for European club football. I applied the same formulas for the pre-game probabilities and for the points exchanged given that probability. The parameters I simply took as given, namely the home advantage, which is set to 100, and the weighting factor k. This parameter puts a weight on the magnitude of the event:

I then stumbled upon an interesting article on pena.lt/y, a site covering sports metrics, with a graph of the relationship between k and the prediction error. The minimum is around k=15. Realising that the weighting factor I used for most games, 40, was not even on the graph, I decided to run my own calculations to see whether I would get similar results.

The two main formulas of the calculation are the two-way win expectancy of the home team, where dr is the difference in ratings before the match,

WEh= 110−dr/400+1

and the points exchanged, with gdm being the goal difference multiplier and outcome taking the values 1 (win), 0.5 (draw) and 0 (loss):

RΔh=k·gdm· (outcome−WEh)

The dynamic between these formulas is clear: given a certain WEh before the game, RΔh changes the post-game ratings of the teams and thereby the WEh of the next game. So k not only alters the size of the rating change, but also the subsequent predictions. A smaller k slows down convergence and avoids very high probabilities for favourites and very low ones for underdogs, which means that upsets are considered more likely – two-way probabilities of 90:10 might become 87:13, for example.

Results

The question then is: does it improve the prediction to assume slightly lower probabilities for the favourite (and higher ones for the underdog) of a given match? To answer it, I apply least squares as suggested by pena.lt/y. The residuals are simply the difference between prediction and outcome,

R=WEh−outcome

and the mean of the squared residuals serves as the measure of the prediction error:

error(k,n)= RSSn

Since the database contained over 470,000 games since 1955 at the time, running the whole history for every specification of k would have taken too long. The analysis is therefore limited to n ∈ {1,000; 5,000; 10,000; 20,000; 30,000; 40,000; 50,000} and k ∈ {5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 40, 50}.

The simulations confirm the main result of pena.lt/y: the error-minimising k is around 15. The size of their dataset is not specified, which is unfortunate, as there is a clear relationship between the size of the dataset and the prediction error: with increasing n the error decreases steadily, and so does the error-minimising k. While the optimum is k=25 and k=20 for the smaller samples of 1,000 and 5,000 games, it is always k=15 for samples between 10,000 and 50,000 games.

The conclusion: the k used was evidently too high and was lowered in the following historical re-runs (see the current values below). The differentiation between types of competition is kept, as teams clearly put a higher value on the Champions League than, say, on domestic competitions – although super cups might deserve less weight, since they often carry no more importance than league cups.

Based on these results, the weighting factors have since been lowered considerably. The differentiation by competition remains; the home advantage is still 100 rating points.

Open questions

Some ideas for further analysis that have not been tackled yet:

Tanking

Quite often, Champions League participants lose a lot of Elo points in a league game right before a Champions League match, supposedly because they field a B team. Since the expected Elo exchange of any given match is zero (teams on average play according to their strength), it would be interesting to test whether this holds for league or cup games directly before a Champions League game.

Scheduling

When this question was first raised in 2017, English clubs had an abundance of money at their disposal but only relatively ordinary success in international competition. It was suggested that their schedule was too tight compared to other countries (no winter break, replays of drawn cup matches, league cup). Conversely, a lighter schedule might explain why Chelsea won the 2016/17 Premier League with relative ease without European football. English clubs have since become far more successful in Europe, and FA Cup replays were scrapped from 2024/25 – but how much schedule density affects results, and whether it should be factored into the ratings, is still an open question.

Rating non-European clubs

Another interesting idea would be to estimate the strength of non-European leagues such as Major League Soccer or the Chinese Super League. Unfortunately, there are few competitive games between European clubs and the rest of the world – essentially the FIFA Club World Cup, which has offered considerably more such matches since its expansion to 32 teams in 2025. One approach would be to use international club friendlies (weighted considerably lower than competitive matches) between European and non-European teams and to find ratings for the non-European teams which minimise the rating changes – small changes suggest that the result was already reflected in the pre-match ratings.
